# SystemKit design system

A quiet learning workspace. Neutral surfaces keep diagrams and explanations in focus, and green and red are reserved for answer feedback.

## Color

| Role | Light | Dark | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Canvas | `#fafafa` | `#0b0b0b` | Page background |
| Surface | `#ffffff` | `#151515` | Reading and question cards |
| Soft surface | `#f5f5f5` | `#222222` | Quiet hover or grouping |
| Primary text and accent | `#171717` | `#f5f5f5` | Headings, focused actions |
| Secondary text | `#404040` | `#d0d0d0` | Body text |
| Muted text | `#595959` | `#b5b5b5` | Supporting labels |
| Accent tint | `#f2f2f2` | `#262626` | Selected or emphasized surfaces |
| Accent line | `#d6d6d6` | `#484848` | Decorative separation only |
| Control border | `#8a8a8a` | `#777777` | Input and control outlines |
| Text on accent | `#ffffff` | `#111111` | Primary button label |
| Success on tint | `#187646` on `#eaf8f0` | `#8bdcae` on `#1a392b` | Correct answers |
| Error on tint | `#9b3e2d` on `#fff3ed` | `#f2a594` on `#482a27` | Incorrect answers |

Body text meets at least 4.5:1 contrast in both themes, and control outlines at least 3:1. Never rely on color alone: keep the label, icon, or explanation.

## Interaction

Give immediate press feedback, with restrained transitions. Keep keyboard focus explicit. Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`, and make translucent chrome solid under `prefers-reduced-transparency`.

## Diagrams

Use one recognizable pictogram per system role, always with a visible label and a short description. Make the direction of a data flow explicit, and give each lesson its own diagram when the architecture differs. Keep the on-page diagram and its Excalidraw source aligned. On narrow screens, keep the full diagram scrollable rather than shrinking labels.

## Pages outside a lesson

The home, chapter, labs, and practice pages share these tokens. List chapters and lessons as ruled rows rather than grids of cards. Headings stand alone, with no small label above them. Show the product working instead of describing it.

## The home page

Milo sits on the course, and the course is the home page's navigation, so the page opens on a single pinned stage rather than a stack of sections. A thin line runs across the stage like a horizon. Where it passes under Milo it is the course: a tick for every lesson in order (every chapter on a phone), with the four parts sharing it equally so even the smallest has room for its name. Finished lessons are inked in, and the next one is the one orange mark on the line. Milo sits over the visitor's next lesson, so a returning learner finds him further along, and what he's thinking starts it: a glass thought bubble, a fully rounded pill floating clear of his ear with a trail of three shrinking glass puffs down to his head, is the page's one main action. The puffs pop out of his head one after another when it appears, then it all floats gently; with reduced motion it simply sits there. The four parts are named in a row under his boots, each under its stretch of the line, and open the course where they point. Pointing along the line names the lesson there and clicking opens it; a tap on a phone shows its chapter first; the arrow keys walk it. The site's four sections sit in a row of glass at the top; on a phone they are a dock at the bottom. Browsing happens in one glass panel that replaces the words beside him: a part, then its chapters, then a chapter's lessons, with the line inking in whatever the panel shows and Milo reading it with the visitor. There is no separate list view, though the list stays in the page for search engines and screen readers, and shows whenever the line can't run.

Scrolling from Milo to what's below dives in through his eye. The camera goes for the eye nearer the pointer (or nearer the middle of the screen), the eye's pupil opens like an iris, and through it is a neuron: through the cell's membrane, down the hillock and along the axon, past a node of Ranvier for each lesson on the real route from the visitor's next lesson to a lesson about scaling, with each lesson's name on its node and side branches for its other links. Milo himself comes with the camera until it is inside: the dive's first frame is an exact copy of him, drawn from the same images the page shows, where the page has put them, so nothing jumps when it takes over. Inside, it looks like an electron micrograph that's moving, in the same neutrals: structure is grey with bright edges, and only the signal glows, as a long front that lights each node's ring of channels as it jumps ahead. The terminal's vesicles flare, its membrane opens on the game's map where its requests start, and the signal carries on as the first request. It's drawn in WebGL at whatever size the device can manage, and anything without WebGL 2, or too slow for it, follows the same route through the course's own lines instead. Inside the dive, one wheel turn, swipe or key press plays all of it, into the eye and out onto the game or all the way back, in about a second, and the rest of that gesture, momentum and all, is held back, so no flick can carry the page past the game. Gestures that start outside the dive scroll the page as usual and are held at the game while they last. Anything else that moves the page, a link or the scroll bar, the dive only follows: it takes at least 0.6 seconds however far the page jumps, and when scrolling stops partway it finishes the way the visitor was going. It loads after the first screen. Before then, without JavaScript, or with reduced motion, there is no dive: the stage fades, or with reduced motion simply scrolls away.

Below the stage, keep words to a minimum and let the working thing make the point. A small game waits there: an app goes viral, and each call changes a live map of the system, with requests moving through it and a big number for how many are failing. When the trouble is the API call rather than a tier, the call itself goes on the map, hanging from the wire it travels along, and a dot's area shows the work in a request. Then four tiles, each with a verb for a title and a chart that draws itself: Break it, Remember it, Estimate it, Design it. A lab's chart runs that lab's own model, so its shape is what the lab really shows. Pick a sample people already wonder about, such as scaling or caching, rather than one that takes explaining.

On the home page, ink means working and the labs' orange means overloaded, with a darker orange for text so it stays readable. Charts sit finished for anyone who prefers reduced motion or has no JavaScript, and they only move while on screen.

### Glass

Glass only appears on the home page, in the same neutrals as everything else. Its tokens are on `.pageHome` in `Site.module.css`. Reach for it where something floats over Milo's stage or the field of soft light behind the page, because that gives the blur something to bend; a glass card on a plain background is just a grey card. Touch screens drop the blur on panels that scroll, leaving it on the lens, the dock, and the sheet. Glass goes solid under `prefers-reduced-transparency` and `prefers-contrast: more`, and blur never carries meaning on its own.

## Labs and charts

Charts use blue for successful work and orange for offered load, with validated light and dark values in `Lab.module.css`. Reference lines are neutral and dashed. Every chart has a legend, and its values are also available as text or a table. Draw charts at their container's real width so labels stay readable on phones.

## Milo

Milo is the site's character: a small robot with a screen for a face, sitting on the course line. He's original to SystemKit, designed from concept art and built in Blender, and he stays in the site's neutrals: white satin in the light theme, charcoal in the dark, with only his eyes and the little lights on his chin and chest glowing. The page shows him as two Cycles renders, his body (with the ledge's contact shadow folded in) and his head, each a WebP of a few tens of kilobytes in each theme, chosen by `prefers-color-scheme`. His face's screen is left blank in the renders and his eyes are drawn on it by the page, so they can blink, look at the pointer or at whatever the page is pointing at, squint happily when the main action is pointed at, and open for the dive. He turns to what he looks at. His eyes dart there first; his head follows a beat later and carries most of the turn, up to 21° to either side and a little up or down, and his torso follows last and least, up to 8°, twisting on hips, legs and fists that stay put on the ledge. A flat image can't turn, so these are real Blender frames: a grid of head turns and nods and a row of torso twists, each layer's frames packed into one sprite (AVIF, with WebP where a browser lacks it) that loads once the page is quiet, so the first screen loads only the straight-ahead layers. A canvas over each layer shows the frames, adding them in their shares so a crossfade is exact; between two frames they crossfade only briefly around the middle, and once he's still the head comes to rest on a real frame, so nothing shows doubled. For each head frame the renders record where his screen lands, so the drawn eyes ride on the turned screen. He breathes with his torso, which rises on still hips while his head rides up on his neck, and now and then he shifts his weight, leaning a little to one side. All of it is a transform, a canvas or a CSS variable; it stops when he's off screen or nothing has happened for 45 seconds, and none of it runs, or loads, for anyone who prefers reduced motion. Where he sits on the line is a transform too, so moving him never shifts the layout.

His shape follows the concept art. His head is tall and round, its cheeks curving round his screen, and its crown grows two thick horn-like ears that curve outward to rounded tips, each with a broad dark panel along it. The face is nearly flat across the screen and curves in one smooth sweep out to the cheeks and the crown. Its screen sits in a soft face plate that is part of the head, bounded by a fine grooved seam that runs round the screen as one symmetric oval, with a grooved chin plate below holding the chin light. Short seams split the forehead under a crown line that runs along the ears' bases. His body is a bell-shaped torso with a flush shield on the chest. It has a dark neck in a collar well, dark shoulder joints set into its sides, and rounded arm shells with a dark elbow joint showing between them. Below the torso, a dark hip ball shows as a crescent between its lower rim and each thigh's rounded top, and each thigh's lower rim arches over a dark knee ball. His fists are white finger segments over a dark palm, and his short legs have dark knees seated in boots whose upright shaft steps down into a domed toe, on dark soles. In the light theme he renders white under broad soft light. In the dark theme he is charcoal, with thin rim light tracing his outline. The visor is dark gloss glass, and the chin and chest lights glow.

**Milo in 3D (prototype).** Once the page is quiet, Milo can instead be drawn live with three.js from his Blender model, so his head and torso turn smoothly to any angle rather than stepping between rendered frames. The model is the sitting pose, cut down to about 150k triangles and Draco-compressed (`public/milo/3d/milo.glb`, about 1 MB), with each part hung on a node at its joint: the head turns on the neck, the torso twists, leans and breathes on still hips, and each arm is solved again every frame so its fist stays on the ledge. To keep the Cycles look, each surface is shaded by a matcap rendered in Cycles for its material, lit by the same studio and seen from the same camera (`matcap-<theme>-<material>.webp`), times the ambient occlusion and the studio lamps' cast shadows baked into the model. The camera frames the same crop box as the image layers, so the 3D canvas fades in exactly over the images, and his eyes stay drawn by the page, carried onto the turned screen by the map between where his screen is straight ahead and where it is now. His contact shadow is its own image (`<theme>-shadow.webp`) under the canvas. Opening the page with `?milo=2d` keeps the rendered turn frames instead, for comparison, and anything without WebGL falls back to them. The model comes from `web.py` and `web_assets.py` in the Milo project, and its camera, joints and screen points are in `src/lib/milo-3d.ts`.

The sources are in the Milo project (`systemkit-milo`). `sitting-turns/Milo-sitting-v3-light.blend` and `-dark.blend` hold the pose, the ledge as a shadow catcher, and the studio light, with every turn frame's pose keyed in the action `Milo turns` (frame 1 is straight ahead; the timeline markers name the frames). The `sitting` folder has the scripts that rebuild them, in order:
1. `head.py`, `body.py` and `hand.py` build the head, the body and the left fist as signed-distance shapes, meshed with marching cubes (they need numpy and scikit-image). Their numbers are in `head.json`, `body.json` and `hand.json`. `body.json`'s `rig` moves the rig's bones to the concept's proportions (longer arms, shoulders tucked in, hips and knees lower); `rig.py` writes the hand bone's frame after that move to `rig.json`, which `hand.py` reads.
2. `turns.sh` moves the bones and puts those meshes on the rig (`shape.py`; `hands.py` mirrors the fist onto the right hand), poses him (`pose.py`, which takes `headYaw`, `headNod` and `torsoTurn` for the frames), applies the studio look from `look-light.json` / `look-dark.json` (his screen mirrors no studio lamp, so it stays black however far he turns), and renders both themes' layers 1800 pixels wide: the straight-ahead body, head and shadow, then the head over a grid of `YAWS` by `NODS` and the torso at each of `TWISTS`. Every frame writes where its eyes, screen and neck land, and the affine map that carries the straight frame's eyes onto it. It renders a few layers per Blender run and tries again on anything missing, so it can be resumed. (`final.sh` still renders only the straight layers, the earlier way.)
3. `KEEP_W=1281 WRITE_TS=1 python3 assets.py ../sitting-turns <repo>` crops every layer and frame into one box (`KEEP_W` keeps it at least as wide as the page was first laid out for, at this size), writes the plain layers and the turn sprites into `public/milo`, and writes the figure geometry to `src/lib/milo-figure.ts` and the frames' to `src/lib/milo-turns.ts`. The sprites' cells are two thirds of the plain layers' size, and the `-s` ones for phones 0.4.

The geometry the page needs from those renders, where his eyes, screen, neck and seat land on the images, is in `src/lib/milo-figure.ts`. Regenerate it with the renders whenever the pose, the camera or his outline changes. The images' aspect ratio, seat and neck are also written into `Milo.module.css`, `MiloHome.module.css` and `CourseNav.module.css`, and so is where his thought bubble's trail meets his head (`--bubble-x` and `--bubble-y` in `MiloHome.module.css`, measured from the head's outline). The images' `sizes` hints in `MiloHome.tsx` and `CourseMap.tsx` are his height on each layout times that aspect ratio. Tests check all of these agree with `milo-figure.ts`, and that the sprites are the size `milo-turns.ts` says.

The chapter list stays in the page for search engines and screen readers, and it shows instead whenever the course line can't run. Anything that can be done on the line can also be done in the panel beside it, in ruled rows. The learning path page uses the same line, with Milo at the next lesson and the shown chapter's lessons beside him.
