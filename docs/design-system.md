# SystemKit design system

A quiet learning workspace. Neutral surfaces keep diagrams and explanations in focus, and green and red are reserved for answer feedback.

## Color

| Role | Light | Dark | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Canvas | `#fafafa` | `#0b0b0b` | Page background |
| Surface | `#ffffff` | `#151515` | Reading and question cards |
| Soft surface | `#f5f5f5` | `#222222` | Quiet hover or grouping |
| Primary text and accent | `#171717` | `#f5f5f5` | Headings, focused actions |
| Secondary text | `#404040` | `#d0d0d0` | Body text |
| Muted text | `#595959` | `#b5b5b5` | Supporting labels |
| Accent tint | `#f2f2f2` | `#262626` | Selected or emphasized surfaces |
| Accent line | `#d6d6d6` | `#484848` | Decorative separation only |
| Control border | `#8a8a8a` | `#777777` | Input and control outlines |
| Text on accent | `#ffffff` | `#111111` | Primary button label |
| Success on tint | `#187646` on `#eaf8f0` | `#8bdcae` on `#1a392b` | Correct answers |
| Error on tint | `#9b3e2d` on `#fff3ed` | `#f2a594` on `#482a27` | Incorrect answers |

Body text meets at least 4.5:1 contrast in both themes, and control outlines at least 3:1. Never rely on color alone: keep the label, icon, or explanation.

## Interaction

Give immediate press feedback, with restrained transitions. Keep keyboard focus explicit. Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`, and make translucent chrome solid under `prefers-reduced-transparency`.

## Diagrams

Use one recognizable pictogram per system role, always with a visible label and a short description. Make the direction of a data flow explicit, and give each lesson its own diagram when the architecture differs. A static diagram keeps its on-page drawing and its Excalidraw source aligned, and on narrow screens stays whole and scrollable rather than shrinking its labels. A lesson with a stage shows it in the static drawing's place, with Steps and 3D as its two views. This is decided per lesson, so a chapter can be half converted: its other lessons keep their drawings.

A lesson stage draws the system from data and plays it step by step. The stage sits on the left and its steps on the right, and each step's text says what the picture shows, with its numbers, what changed, and a control where the step has one, so the text is the stage's text equivalent; under 860px the steps go below the stage and only the current one shows. A playback bar floats at the stage's foot with reset, back, play and next, and a pip for each step (a count on a phone). Every step has its own link (`#step-3`) and is announced to screen readers; Space or K plays and pauses, the arrow keys step, Escape pauses, Home resets, the number keys jump, and a sideways swipe steps on a touch screen. Tiers run left to right, or top to bottom on a phone, and tags, pills and messages keep their size however far the camera zooms. Nothing moves while the stage is off screen.

Each change plays in order: parts that go fade and sink away, parts that stay move to their new places, new parts drop in from above one after another with a small overshoot and a ring where they land, and only then do the links between them draw themselves. The camera moves only when the next scene doesn't fit the view it already has. Links lie on the floor like cables: they run along the floor's two axes, the lines of the dot grid, and on a phone also straight down it, its third direction (`src/lib/lesson-stage/routing.ts`). A link plugs into a small port on the front of a part's base where the part's name tag leaves room. Where the tag covers the front it leaves from behind the part, and on a phone, where each tier hangs under the one before, it leaves from under the tag and comes in behind the part below. Links keep clear of other parts, their tags and load pills. Links that leave a face together fan out 8px apart in the order they turn off, so they never cross each other there, and where one link crosses another the one out of focus, or else the later one, breaks to pass under it. Each link takes its shape from where the parts are going, so it keeps that shape while they move. Messages travel as small labelled pills, each on its own lane so a request and its reply never overlap. A pill stops short of the part it reaches and fades as its trail runs on into the port. Ink is a request, the labs' blue (`--work`) is completed work and replies, and the labs' orange (`--hot`) is kept for overload or failure: a part running hot, a part that's down, a message that's lost. The numbers over the stage count to their new value when a step changes them. With reduced motion each step is a still picture, its routes drawn and its messages resting short of where they arrive, clear of the parts, their notes and each other where the link has room, with nothing travelling.

The pictograms are real objects drawn in code, each one whose working is the idea (a breaker trips, a lease runs down like a parking meter), in the page's neutrals: an ink silhouette over faint creases, four close face tones with a bevel of light under each top edge, smooth shading on round parts, and recessed, screwed and slotted details in the muted tone, all coloured from page tokens so both themes work. A core set loads with every stage and the other parts come in packs a lesson loads only when it uses them; `docs/pictogram-catalog.md` lists every part and the options each reads. Each stands on a rounded base whose front carries its one status light, grey when idle, ink when on, blue for work and orange when hot. Its name tag sits on the front of the base, and its load pill beside the part on a wide screen and above it on a phone. Scenes live in `src/content/lesson-stage/<chapter>/<lesson>.ts`, and a lesson plays as a stage once its id is listed in that folder's `index.ts` and has its line in `player.tsx`. `node scripts/stage-check.cjs <chapter>` checks the listed scenes, and content-check fails a chapter when a listed scene is missing, doesn't validate, or isn't wired into `player.tsx`, or when a scene file isn't listed.

## Pages outside a lesson

The home, chapter, labs, and practice pages share these tokens. List chapters and lessons as ruled rows rather than grids of cards. Headings stand alone, with no small label above them. Show the product working instead of describing it.

## The home page

Milo sits on the course, and the course is the home page's navigation, so the page opens on a single pinned stage rather than a stack of sections. The stage is a place: a wall, and a ledge running across it that Milo sits on, his boots hanging over its front. The ledge is drawn in the page's neutrals as a solid slab seen from a little above: its top going back to the wall, darker in the corner where they meet, a front edge that catches the light, a front face, and its soft shadow on the wall under it. That lit front edge is the course's line. Along it the face is marked like a ruler, the marks cut into it so each catches a little light: a tick for every lesson in order (every chapter on a phone), with the four parts sharing it equally so even the smallest has room for its name. Finished lessons are inked in, and the next one is the one orange mark on the line: a dab of the wall's paint on the ledge's front edge, with a short run down its face ending in a rounded head, which slides along the ledge with Milo when the next lesson changes. With forced colours it's the plain tick again. The learning path page, which has no wall, keeps the plain orange tick. Milo is set off from the wall behind him: in the light theme by the soft shadow he casts on it, and in the dark by soft light on the wall behind him, both following him along the ledge and stopping at its back edge. On a phone held sideways the ledge comes out of the light beside the words rather than stopping short.

Orange paint comes over the top of the wall and runs down it: one short spill, a few glossy runs of different lengths with rounded heads, in the labs' orange (`--hot-line`), which is the one colour that pops. It shows that the space above the ledge is a wall behind Milo. On a wide screen it hangs between the words and Milo; on a phone it comes down the right-hand edge, past the words and behind his thought, to beside him. It's drawn as SVG from `src/lib/paint.ts`, its outline lit by an SVG lighting filter, so the sheet and its runs read as one thick surface with a soft shadow on the wall, and a sharp glint down each run and a spark on each head make it look wet. The longest run is still creeping: over a minute after the page loads it slides a little further, slowing as it goes, in small steps so the wall is redrawn only a few times a second. It waits while the wall is off screen and doesn't move at all for anyone who prefers reduced motion. With forced colours the paint and the light on the wall go and the ledge is just its edge; with more contrast the ledge's edges are drawn as firmly as a control's. Milo sits over the visitor's next lesson, so a returning learner finds him further along, and what he's thinking starts it: a glass thought bubble, a fully rounded pill floating clear of his ear with a trail of three shrinking glass puffs down to his head, is the page's one main action. The puffs pop out of his head one after another when it appears, then it all floats gently; with reduced motion it simply sits there. The four parts are named in a row under his boots, each under its stretch of the line, and open the course where they point. Pointing along the line names the lesson there and clicking opens it; a tap on a phone shows its chapter first; the arrow keys walk it. The site's four sections sit in a row of glass at the top; on a phone, upright or on its side, they sit in a row beside the brand's mark, which keeps only its mark, and scroll away with the first screen. On a phone on its side, browsing takes the words' place in the left column, with Milo beside it. The home page runs edge to edge (`viewport-fit=cover`): its soft light, the ledge and the dive fill the room beside a phone's notch, while everything to read or touch keeps inside the safe area. A tap answers with the page's own press, never the browser's grey flash, and hover looks are only for a pointer that hovers, since a touch screen would keep them after the tap. Browsing happens in one glass panel that replaces the words beside him: a part, then its chapters, then a chapter's lessons, with the line inking in whatever the panel shows and Milo reading it with the visitor. There is no separate list view, though the list stays in the page for search engines and screen readers, and shows whenever the line can't run.

On a screen held upright, a phone or a tablet, there's height to spare and little width, so Milo hangs on the side of the course instead of sitting on it: the line becomes a thin round rod running up the screen's left edge, lit like the ledge and in the ledge's neutrals, bent back into the wall at each end. The course runs up it, the first lesson at its foot, with a tick for every lesson cut into its left side in the margin. Milo hangs off its right side as a real body would: his near fist grips the rod above his shoulder, his arm straight and taking his weight with the elbow just soft, his body leaning out from it with the sole of his near boot braced against the rod lower down, as you'd swing out from a lamppost, and his free arm and leg hanging straight down. His head tilts a little away from his raised arm. He's large and stays in one comfortable place: the rod slides so the visitor's next lesson, its dab of paint just under his fist, is always where he holds on, so he climbs as the visitor goes on, and a new visitor sees the rod's foot under his boots with the whole course rising above him. Only a stretch of the rod fits the screen, so the whole course stands in small on the wall to his right, from the top of his head to his knees: a thin rod of its own with the four parts up it, each stretch as long as its share of the lessons, the first at the foot as on the big rod, each named to its right with its number and lesson count. Finished stretches are inked in, the visitor's place is the same orange dab as on the big rod, and the part they're in is in ink while the rest stay grey. A part's name opens it in the sheet, and the small rod fades while the sheet is open, since the sheet has its own way between parts. The first screen is just the words and the rod, and the four sections sit in a row of glass at the top beside the brand's mark, scrolling away with it, so the only bar along the bottom is the browser's own. Safari draws the page on under that bar, down past what it counts as the screen, so while the page sits at the very top the game below is kept out of sight, and it comes in with the first few pixels of scroll while it's still behind the bar; the game still starts close under the first screen. On a phone held upright that row would crowd the top, so the brand keeps its name and a small glass menu button sits across from it; tapping it rolls a glass wheel in from the right edge, its hub on the edge, while the page behind blurs a little. The sections stand on its spokes, read from the rim inward so they tip as the wheel turns, with ticks round the rim for the eye to follow. The one at nine o'clock, against a notch on the rim and in a lens of glass, is in hand; the notch and the lens's outline are the page's orange, the one touch of it on the wheel, so the eye finds the section in hand at once. A finger turns the wheel with momentum and it settles on a section, a tap on any section opens it (Learn opens the course in the sheet, as everywhere on this page), and a tap off the wheel, the cross where the menu button was, or Escape puts it away. It's a real modal dialog, so the page behind can't be reached while it's open, and the dive takes no gesture made on it; the paint spill on the wall goes, leaving the dab as the one orange on the page itself. A tap on the rod opens its chapter in a sheet from the bottom, and as it opens the rod and Milo rise into the words' place so his face stays above it, reading along; the sheet takes no tap for its first moment, so a second tap can't open what's under the finger. Choosing what the visitor is here for moves into the sheet. Hanging, he's two Cycles renders like the sitting ones, posed on the same rig (the Milo project's `hanging/` scripts), with the rod cut out of them wherever it passes in front of him so the page's own rod shows through his fingers; his eyes are drawn the same way, turned with his tilted screen, and he looks with his eyes, his head tipping a degree or two toward where he looks. He arrives as if he'd just caught the rod, a small drop his arm takes up, rather than fading in. The 3D model never loads here.

Scrolling from Milo to what's below dives in through his eye. The camera goes for the eye nearer the pointer (or nearer the middle of the screen), the eye's pupil opens like an iris, and through it is a neuron: through the cell's membrane, down the hillock and along the axon, past a node of Ranvier for each lesson on the real route from the visitor's next lesson to a lesson about scaling, with each lesson's name on its node and side branches for its other links. Milo himself comes with the camera until it is inside: the dive's first frame is an exact copy of him, drawn from the same images the page shows, where the page has put them, so nothing jumps when it takes over. Inside, it looks like an electron micrograph that's moving, in the same neutrals: structure is grey with bright edges, and only the signal glows, as a long front that lights each node's ring of channels as it jumps ahead. The terminal's vesicles flare, its membrane opens on the game's map where its requests start, and the signal carries on as the first request. It's drawn in WebGL at whatever size the device can manage, and anything without WebGL 2, or too slow for it, follows the same route through the course's own lines instead. The dive takes no gesture for itself: a wheel, a swipe or a key scrolls the page as it would anywhere, and the dive follows the page, so a slow drag plays it slowly under the finger and a flick goes straight through it, past the game if that's where the flick goes. When scrolling stops partway, the dive finishes the way the visitor was going once they're a third of the way in, and otherwise springs back, so a nudge past Milo's line doesn't play the whole of it. A link or the scroll bar jumping the page is followed too, taking at least 0.6 seconds however far the page jumps. It plays with the scroll once, on a visitor's first visit: once it has reached the game, the browser remembers, and from then on the page scrolls past Milo as it would without the dive, the stage fading as it goes. A tap or click on Milo's face plays it again, down to the game, on any visit, and the pointer turns to a hand over his face to say so. It loads after the first screen, and only on a screen at least 960px wide. A phone, upright or on its side, scrolls straight from Milo to the game: there the dive would take a visitor's first swipe, most swipes are flicks that would show it as a blur, and the lessons on its nodes are too small to read. Before it loads, without JavaScript, or with reduced motion, there is no dive: the stage fades, or with reduced motion simply scrolls away.

Below the stage, keep words to a minimum and let the working thing make the point. A small game waits there: an app goes viral, and each call changes a live map of the system, with requests moving through it and a big number for how many are failing. Then four tiles, each with a verb for a title and a chart that draws itself: Break it, Remember it, Estimate it, Design it. A lab's chart runs that lab's own model, so its shape is what the lab really shows. Pick a sample people already wonder about, such as scaling or caching, rather than one that takes explaining.

On a narrow screen the game's map stands up: requests come in at the top and fall through the tiers to the database at the foot, down a line through the middle of the card with each tier named to its right. The map takes the height the screen has left beside the rest of the card, measured against Safari with its bars showing, so the system has room to read and the card and its choices still fit on one screen.

On the home page, ink means working and the labs' orange means overloaded, with a darker orange for text so it stays readable. Charts sit finished for anyone who prefers reduced motion or has no JavaScript, and they only move while on screen.

### Glass

Glass appears on the home page and on a lesson stage's playback bar, in the same neutrals as everything else. The home page's tokens are on `.pageHome` in `Site.module.css`, and the stage's on its root in `LessonStage.module.css`. Reach for it where something floats over Milo's stage, the field of soft light behind the page, or a lesson stage's drawing, because that gives the blur something to bend; a glass card on a plain background is just a grey card. Touch screens drop the blur on panels that scroll, leaving it on the lens, the dock, and the sheet. Glass goes solid under `prefers-reduced-transparency` and `prefers-contrast: more`, the playback bar included, and blur never carries meaning on its own.

## Labs and charts

Charts use blue for successful work and orange for offered load, with validated light and dark values in `Lab.module.css`. Reference lines are neutral and dashed. Every chart has a legend, and its values are also available as text or a table. Draw charts at their container's real width so labels stay readable on phones.

## Milo

Milo is the site's character: a small robot with a screen for a face, sitting on the ledge that is the course. He's original to SystemKit, designed from concept art and built in Blender, and he stays in the site's neutrals: white satin in the light theme, charcoal in the dark, with only his eyes and the little lights on his chin and chest glowing. The page shows him as two Cycles renders, his body (with the ledge's contact shadow folded in) and his head, each a WebP of a few tens of kilobytes in each theme, chosen by `prefers-color-scheme`. His face's screen is left blank in the renders and his eyes are drawn on it by the page, so they can blink, look at the pointer or at whatever the page is pointing at, squint happily when the main action is pointed at, and open for the dive. He turns to what he looks at. His eyes dart there first; his head follows a beat later and carries most of the turn, up to 21° to either side and a little up or down, and his torso follows last and least, up to 8°, twisting on hips, legs and fists that stay put on the ledge. A flat image can't turn, so these are real Blender frames: a grid of head turns and nods and a row of torso twists, each layer's frames packed into one sprite (AVIF, with WebP where a browser lacks it) that loads once the page is quiet, so the first screen loads only the straight-ahead layers. A canvas over each layer shows the frames, adding them in their shares so a crossfade is exact; between two frames they crossfade only briefly around the middle, and once he's still the head comes to rest on a real frame, so nothing shows doubled. For each head frame the renders record where his screen lands, so the drawn eyes ride on the turned screen. He breathes with his torso, which rises on still hips while his head rides up on his neck, and now and then he shifts his weight, leaning a little to one side. All of it is a transform, a canvas or a CSS variable; it stops when he's off screen or nothing has happened for 45 seconds, and none of it runs, or loads, for anyone who prefers reduced motion. Where he sits on the line is a transform too, so moving him never shifts the layout.

His shape follows the concept art. His head is tall and round, its cheeks curving round his screen, and its crown grows two thick horn-like ears that curve outward to rounded tips, each with a broad dark panel along it. The face is nearly flat across the screen and curves in one smooth sweep out to the cheeks and the crown. Its screen sits in a soft face plate that is part of the head, bounded by a fine grooved seam that runs round the screen as one symmetric oval, with a grooved chin plate below holding the chin light. Short seams split the forehead under a crown line that runs along the ears' bases. His body is a bell-shaped torso with a flush shield on the chest. It has a dark neck in a collar well, dark shoulder joints set into its sides, and rounded arm shells with a dark elbow joint showing between them. Below the torso, a dark hip ball shows as a crescent between its lower rim and each thigh's rounded top, and each thigh's lower rim arches over a dark knee ball. His fists are white finger segments over a dark palm, and his short legs have dark knees seated in boots whose upright shaft steps down into a domed toe, on dark soles. In the light theme he renders white under broad soft light. In the dark theme he is charcoal, with thin rim light tracing his outline. The visor is dark gloss glass, and the chin and chest lights glow.

**Milo in 3D (prototype).** Once the page is quiet, Milo can instead be drawn live with three.js from his Blender model, so his head and torso turn smoothly to any angle rather than stepping between rendered frames. The model is the sitting pose, cut down to about 150k triangles and Draco-compressed (`public/milo/3d/milo.glb`, about 1.2 MB), with each part hung on a node at its joint: the head turns on the neck, the torso twists, leans and breathes on still hips, and each arm is solved again every frame so its fist stays on the ledge. To keep the Cycles look, each surface starts from a matcap rendered in Cycles for its material, lit by the same studio and seen from the same camera (`matcap-<theme>-<material>.webp`). The matcaps hold display colours, after Blender's colour transform (AgX with the theme's look and exposure), so the page undoes that transform with a table sampled from Blender, lights in scene values, and redoes it, so a white in shadow rolls off softly as in the renders instead of turning grey. In scene values, each vertex's diffuse light is corrected by what Cycles baked into the model for each theme: how much more or less light it really gets than a lone sphere of its material facing the same way, which takes in shadow, occlusion, the shell's own contact darkening, and the light his white parts throw on each other. Glossy highlights stay as the matcap has them, and each matcap's rim is carried out to its corners so his outline doesn't pick up the empty background. The camera frames the same crop box as the image layers, so the 3D canvas fades in exactly over the images, and his eyes stay drawn by the page, carried onto the turned screen by the map between where his screen is straight ahead and where it is now. His contact shadow is its own image (`<theme>-shadow.webp`) under the canvas. Opening the page with `?milo=2d` keeps the rendered turn frames instead, for comparison, and anything without WebGL falls back to them. The model comes from `web.sh` (which runs `web.py`'s steps) and `web_assets.py` in the Milo project, and its camera, joints, screen points and colour transform tables are in `src/lib/milo-3d.ts`.

The sources are in the Milo project (`systemkit-milo`). `sitting-turns/Milo-sitting-v3-light.blend` and `-dark.blend` hold the pose, the ledge as a shadow catcher, and the studio light, with every turn frame's pose keyed in the action `Milo turns` (frame 1 is straight ahead; the timeline markers name the frames). The `sitting` folder has the scripts that rebuild them, in order:
1. `head.py`, `body.py` and `hand.py` build the head, the body and the left fist as signed-distance shapes, meshed with marching cubes (they need numpy and scikit-image). Their numbers are in `head.json`, `body.json` and `hand.json`. `body.json`'s `rig` moves the rig's bones to the concept's proportions (longer arms, shoulders tucked in, hips and knees lower); `rig.py` writes the hand bone's frame after that move to `rig.json`, which `hand.py` reads.
2. `turns.sh` moves the bones and puts those meshes on the rig (`shape.py`; `hands.py` mirrors the fist onto the right hand), poses him (`pose.py`, which takes `headYaw`, `headNod` and `torsoTurn` for the frames), applies the studio look from `look-light.json` / `look-dark.json` (his screen mirrors no studio lamp, so it stays black however far he turns), and renders both themes' layers 1800 pixels wide: the straight-ahead body, head and shadow, then the head over a grid of `YAWS` by `NODS` and the torso at each of `TWISTS`. Every frame writes where its eyes, screen and neck land, and the affine map that carries the straight frame's eyes onto it. It renders a few layers per Blender run and tries again on anything missing, so it can be resumed. (`final.sh` still renders only the straight layers, the earlier way.)
3. `KEEP_W=1281 WRITE_TS=1 python3 assets.py ../sitting-turns <repo>` crops every layer and frame into one box (`KEEP_W` keeps it at least as wide as the page was first laid out for, at this size), writes the plain layers and the turn sprites into `public/milo`, and writes the figure geometry to `src/lib/milo-figure.ts` and the frames' to `src/lib/milo-turns.ts`. The sprites' cells are two thirds of the plain layers' size, and the `-s` ones for phones 0.4.

The geometry the page needs from those renders, where his eyes, screen, neck and seat land on the images, is in `src/lib/milo-figure.ts`. Regenerate it with the renders whenever the pose, the camera or his outline changes. The images' aspect ratio, seat and neck are also written into `Milo.module.css`, `MiloHome.module.css` and `CourseNav.module.css`, and so is where his thought bubble's trail meets his head (`--bubble-x` and `--bubble-y` in `MiloHome.module.css`, measured from the head's outline). The images' `sizes` hints in `MiloHome.tsx` and `CourseMap.tsx` are his height on each layout times that aspect ratio. Tests check all of these agree with `milo-figure.ts`, and that the sprites are the size `milo-turns.ts` says.

The chapter list stays in the page for search engines and screen readers, and it shows instead whenever the course line can't run. Anything that can be done on the line can also be done in the panel beside it, in ruled rows. The learning path page uses the same line, with Milo at the next lesson and the shown chapter's lessons beside him.
